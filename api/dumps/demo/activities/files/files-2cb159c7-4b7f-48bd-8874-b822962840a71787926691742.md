Relational databases guarantee **ACID properties**, which are desirable, but in the era of **Big Data** comes the need to **adopt technologies that scale better**. NoSQL databases **handle big and complex data** in a **more efficient way**. ACID is not guaranteed, but **BASE properties** work even **better** for a **wide number of applications**. Let's understand why.

#### ACID Properties of RDBMSs

**Relational databases** are still used, and actually, they are **perfectly fine for a large number of applications**. In RDBMSs, **data is stored according to the _relational model_**, in which data is represented **in the form of tables** (relations) **connected together using keys** (Primary Keys and Foreign Keys).

**The advantage** of using RDBMS resides in the **ACID properties**:

- **_A_**_tomicity:_ **Transactions** are considered **single units**. If an operation fails, the entire transaction is rolled back.
- **_C_**_onsistency:_ **Transactions that break consistency** are not allowed and **will fail**.
- **_I_**_solation:_ **Transactions cannot occur at the same time**. Imagine a sort of “traffic light” to access data.
- **_D_**_urability:_ In case of failure, it’s possible to **restore committed transactions** thanks to backups and logs.

**ACID properties** are generally desired but **come at the cost of flexibility and scalability.**


#### Major Problems of RDBMSs

If **RDBMSs** have different advantages, they also **have** **some disadvantages**. First of all, **RDBMSs are not flexible**: modifying the schema is hard, and if **data is rapidly evolving,** this could pose a **problem**.

Another point regards **semi-structured data** and **complex objects**. In fact, **data** that is **not** in the **conventional tabular** or **relational form** cannot be stored in RDBMSs and **requires a different storage solution**.

In the end, **RDBMSs don’t scale well** due to ACID properties.

So, what can we do to **overcome these problems**? **NoSQL databases come to help**, allowing for **scalability** and **flexibility**.


3. **_NoSQL Databases_**

NoSQL, non-relational, schema-free, non-acid, and horizontal scalable are all synonyms indicating **storage solutions not based on the _relational model_**. These solutions, therefore, **do not use tabular relationships**.

There are **four categories** of NoSQL databases:

1. **_Key-Value Store:_** Simply store **key-value pairs**. The API generally provides `put()` , `get()` and `delete()` functionalities. Examples are Memcache and Redis.
2. **_Columns Store:_** Here, data is **stored by columns** rather than by rows. This allows for **data** **compression** and **faster reading speed**. Examples are Bigtable and Cassandra.
3. **_Document Store:_** In these databases, values are **whole documents**. Data is generally stored in **JSON** or **BSON**. Examples are CouchDB and MongoDB.
4. **_Graph Databases:_** These databases use **nodes**, **edges**, and **properties**. **Nodes** (entities) **are connected with edges** that represent the **relation between the nodes**. **Properties** are used to **describe nodes or relationships**. Graph databases are best suited for **heavily-interconnected data**.

This list is **ordered based on the complexity of data** the NoSQL solution can handle. In particular, a key-value solution can simply store key-value pairs, while a graph database can handle **complex networks**.


4. **_The CAP Theorem of Databases_**

The CAP theorem, by Eric Brewer, states that it's **impossible for a database system to achieve Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance at the same time**. In particular, **we can achieve**:

- **_CA:_** Consistency & Availability OR
- **_CP:_** Consistency & Partition Tolerance OR
- **_AP:_** Availability & Partition Tolerance

Where these properties are **defined by**:

- **_C_**_onsistency:_ **Correctness** of data.
- **_A_**_vailability:_ Every request **receives a response** (always on).
- **_P_**_artition Tolerance:_ **Fault-tolerant** distributed environment (the system keeps operating).

While **RDBMSs** are on the **Consistency & Availability** side, **NoSQL** databases allow for **availability and partition tolerance** (AP).

The **AP model** reflects the **BASE properties** of the NoSQL databases.

5. **_BASE Properties of NoSQL Databases_**

If ACID properties belong to RDBMSs, **BASE properties belong to NoSQL** databases. As I said in the last section, sometimes we **prefer availability at the cost of consistency**. In particular, **BASE stands for**:

- **_B_**_asically,_ **_A_**_vailable:_ The system **works all the time** (available in case of failure).
- **_S_**_oft State:_ The data source **does not have to be consistent** all the time.
- **_E_**_ventually Consistent:_ Not immediately consistent, but **at some point**, data **will be consistent**—no guarantee about when this will occur.

BASE properties are preferred in systems where the **ACID model is overkill**.

6. **_SQL vs. NoSQL: How to Choose?_**

There is **not a clear-cut answer** to this question.

**Depending on the business model** and the **application domain**, one choice can be **more suited than another**. Generally speaking, if you need a **fast** & **flexible** & **scalable** environment, **go for NoSQL** solutions.

If you need **consistency** and prefer a **rigid structure**, then **go for a relational** solution.

Remember that **sometimes you don’t need to choose**: lots of systems use **both SQL and NoSQL** solutions in their architectures.
